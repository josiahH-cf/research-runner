## Complete fiction

Odalys Feregrino had piloted ships through the Cut for twenty-six years, and in that time she had made herself a rule: she never learned where they had come from, and she never found out where they went. The rule was not modesty. It was hygiene. A pilot who carried every ship's whole voyage in her head would drown in it before she was forty.

The call came a little after nine, the way it always did in the fog season — the harbormaster's voice thinned by static, giving her a name, a draft, a berth. *Verano*, out of Progreso, grain in the holds, riding low. First-time master. Tide turns at 1:52.

She dressed without hurrying. There was no version of this work that rewarded hurry. By the time she reached the station the fog had already come up off the river the way it did most nights in October, not a wall but a thickening, so that the lights of Sallow Point didn't so much disappear as lose their edges. Renato had the cutter warm at the dock, its running lights smudged to soft coronas in the wet air.

"Bad one?" he asked, the way he always asked, and she gave him the answer she always gave.

"We'll see when we see her."

*Slack Water* took them out past the jetty light and into the roads, engine steady under the deck plates, Renato easing back on the throttle as the freighter's shape resolved out of the murk — high sides streaked with rust at the scuppers, deck lights haloed, a scramble of shadow at the rail where someone was already lowering the ladder. Odalys had climbed a thousand ladders like it. She still checked it the way she'd been taught: eyed the lashings, watched it swing, waited for the roll of the hull to settle into its rhythm before she stepped on. A pilot who got sloppy about the ladder didn't get the chance to get sloppy about anything else.

The climb was forty feet in weather that made the rungs slick, and she took it without letting her attention leave her hands and feet until she came over the rail onto the *Verano*'s deck, where a man was waiting for her with a light that trembled slightly, aimed a little too low, the beam bouncing across her boots instead of her face.

"Pilot," he said. "Thank God."

"Captain Lima?"

"Teodoro." He caught himself. "Captain Lima. Yes."

She logged him without seeming to: young, younger than the ship, a neat uniform still creased from its packaging, hands that wanted something to hold. First command. She had met a hundred of him. Some of them hardened by the end of the passage into men who nodded at her once and never thought of her again, which was correct, which was the point. Some of them never came back through the Cut at all, and she did not ask why, because it was none of her business, and because the asking was the first loose thread that unraveled the whole discipline.

"We have a fault in the number two engine," he said, walking her toward the bridge faster than the deck plates liked. "Intermittent. The chief says it clears on its own. I don't like it."

"Neither do I, until I know what it is." She said it mildly. Fear was a fuel and it needed a use, not a scolding. "Show me your chart."

On the bridge the second officer had already marked the line she would give him, a habit of hope more than preparation, and she let it stand until she'd walked the actual water in her head — the bar shifted every autumn, she'd taken soundings herself in September, and this year the deep water had crept west, the way it did when the summer's storms ran heavy from the gulf. She redrew the line herself, in a hand steadier than the one that had drawn the first, and did not comment on the difference.

"The channel's narrower than it was in spring," she said. "We'll favor the red side by half again what your chart shows. If the fault takes the engine at the wrong moment, I want you already committed to the turn, not still deciding on it."

"Yes." He swallowed. "Yes, Pilot."

"Odalys," she said, and immediately wondered why she'd said it, because she never did. It cost her nothing and she gave it to almost no one, not from coldness but because a name was a hook and she preferred not to be caught on anyone's memory longer than the transit required. She saw it land in him anyway, a small settling, the kind of thing frightened men take gratefully whether or not it was offered as comfort.

They got underway at eleven.

The fog thickened rather than lifted as they came up on the outer buoys, and she called the courses from memory and from the loom of the shore lights she'd used for two and a half decades, the church tower at Punta Seca a paler smear at two points off the bow, the old quarantine light blinking its four-second character through the murk like something breathing under a blanket. She had, by now, given up trying to explain to anyone what this felt like, this holding of an entire moving system — the ship's swing, the current's push, the shoaling water, the turning tide — in a single continuous attention that had no room in it for anything else. It was not thinking, exactly. It was closer to what she imagined perfect pitch might be: not an answer arrived at, but a wrongness that would announce itself the instant it existed.

"Fifteen degrees to port," she said, and felt Teodoro Lima's whole body want to argue with her, the natural mutiny of a man watching his ship turn toward water his own chart called shallow, and to his credit he only relayed the order and stood very still while it was carried out.

The fault took the engine at nine minutes past midnight, exactly where she had told him it would matter most, in the elbow of the Cut where the channel doglegs around the old ballast shoal. The deck note changed under her feet before anyone spoke — a missed beat, a stagger — and she heard the second officer's breath catch and did not let herself feel the same catch in her own chest, because there was no room for it.

"Chief," Teodoro said into the handset, too high.

"Tell him to bleed the secondary fuel line before he touches anything else," Odalys said, already turning to the helmsman. "Rudder amidships. Hold your swing on the current, don't fight it — we have room on this side for eleven more seconds before we don't." She had not looked at a clock. She did not need to.

The engine caught again on the ninth second, not the eleventh, and the ship came through the dogleg the way a big, badly frightened animal comes through a gate it didn't trust — gracelessly, but through. On the bridge nobody spoke for a moment that was longer than it needed to be.

"How," Teodoro said finally, "did you know it had eleven seconds."

"I didn't know," she said. "I knew what I could still do with ten, and I stopped counting to be exact about the eleventh." It was not quite the whole truth, and it was not quite a lie. The truth was simpler and stranger than either: she had never, in twenty-six years, let herself learn what happened when the count ran out, because ships that ran out of room did not come back through the Cut for her to pilot again, and she preferred her knowledge assembled entirely out of passages that had ended.

They cleared the bar forty minutes before the tide went slack, which was as fine a margin as she liked to work with and finer than she liked to admit to a first-time captain. The fog thinned as they came into the lee of the point, the way it always did past the Cut, as if the difficult water and the difficult air belonged to each other and ended together. Ahead, the inner harbor's lights lay in their long familiar rows, refineries and grain elevators and the sodium glow over the container berths, all of it close enough now that Teodoro Lima's shoulders had begun, visibly, to come down from around his ears.

This was the part of the work that never got any easier to explain to people on land: the last hour belonged to someone else. The channel eased, the water opened, and everything that had required her total and undivided attention became, mile by mile, merely nautical, merely competent, a thing any reasonably careful officer could finish. She would ride along another twenty minutes as courtesy and insurance both, and then she would be put back into the dark she'd come out of, and the ship would go on into the port without her, and by morning it would be a shape at a berth with a manifest and a customs stamp and no memory at all of the woman who had told it where to put its rudder at nine minutes past midnight.

She had made peace with this so long ago that she rarely noticed making it anymore. It was not a wound. It was closer to a diet: something she had decided, once, to live inside, because the alternative — following every ship all the way to wherever it was going, keeping track of whether the fault in the number two engine ever got properly repaired, whether the grain sold for enough to matter, whether Teodoro Lima made captain in truth and not just in name — was not a kindness to herself, and she had come, slowly, over a great many transits, to understand that it was not even a kindness to the ships. What she owed them was the Cut. Not the story after it.

"Pilot boat's coming up on the port side," Renato's voice said in her ear, tinny through the handset, right on schedule.

She went to find Teodoro Lima on the bridge wing, where he had gone to watch the harbor lights the way young captains always did after their first hard passage, half disbelieving they'd been allowed to keep the ship.

"You have easy water from here," she told him. "Follow the buoys in, mind the ferry traffic off the coal pier, and get that engine looked at properly before you take her out again, not just bled and blessed."

"I will." He turned from the rail. In the wheelhouse light his face looked younger than it had at the ladder, as if the last three hours had used up all the age he'd borrowed to get the command in the first place. "Pilot — I have to log you. Ship's articles. I never got—" He stopped, embarrassed at himself for not asking sooner, for treating a person for three hours as a function of the fog. "Your name. For the record."

It was a small, ordinary, entirely bureaucratic request. Every pilot's name went into every ship's log; it was one of the few permanent traces this work left anywhere, a line of ink in a book she would never see again, on a ship whose fate she had already decided not to follow. She had given her name to hundreds of logs this way and thought no more of it than of signing for a delivery. That was the whole design of the thing: the record persisted; she did not have to.

And yet she found herself standing at the rail a beat longer than the question required, the pilot ladder already going down over the side into the cutter's spotlight, Renato's engine idling below her like a held breath, and she understood, with a small, unwelcome clarity, that what had unsettled her tonight was not the fault in the engine and not even the eleven seconds. It was that Teodoro Lima, badly frightened and trying not to show it, asking too many questions and thanking her too many times, had reminded her for three hours of her own son, who was somewhere at sea himself now on a ship she had not asked the name of, under a master she had never met, and whom she had also, by the same discipline, agreed not to follow past the edge of what she could actually do for him.

"Odalys Feregrino," she said, and watched him write it, the pen scratching once, twice, the letters going down into the permanent part of the world in a way that nothing else about the last three hours would. "Two r's. Two e's."

"Thank you," he said, and it was clear he meant the whole night, not the spelling.

She went down the ladder the way she always did, facing out, timing the cutter's roll, and did not look back up until her boots were on *Slack Water*'s deck and the *Verano* was already a shape rather than a ship, her running lights withdrawing into the harbor's larger, indifferent glow. Renato brought the cutter around, opened the throttle, and the fog that had thinned in the lee of the point closed softly back in around them as they turned for home, the way it did every night, erasing the channel behind her exactly as thoroughly as it had ever hidden it ahead.

She did not, as a rule, watch a ship all the way to its berth. Tonight, for exactly as long as the lights stayed visible through the murk — not a minute more, she told herself, and then two, and then let herself stop counting — she watched anyway, and allowed herself, once, the small, undisciplined luxury of wondering what would happen to it.

Then the harbor took the *Verano* the rest of the way in without her, as it always did, and Odalys Feregrino turned her face forward into the fog, toward the work still waiting for her at the mouth of the Cut, and let the not-knowing close back over her like water finding its level.
